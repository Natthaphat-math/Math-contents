Linear algebra · probability · optimisation

The math that picks what you see next.

A feed is not magic and it is not a mind reader. It is a pile of arithmetic run over a few billion candidates, several hundred times a second. This page takes that arithmetic apart — and lets you turn every knob in it.

10 interactive sections every number computed live no network, no libraries drag · slide · step · reseed

Instagram and TikTok have never published their ranking systems, and this page does not pretend to know them. What is public is the family of techniques the whole industry builds on — described in research papers and engineering blog posts from Google, Meta, Netflix, Spotify and ByteDance. Those techniques are what you will meet here.

Every model below is a toy: small enough to watch, real enough that the arithmetic is genuinely being done in your browser as you move the controls. Where a toy departs from production reality, there is a note saying so. Two colours run through the page: amber is you — your actions, your taste, the things you control — and cyan is the system — its estimates, its scores, its guesses about you.

01

Three billion candidates, twenty slots

Why no feed ever scores everything with its best model — and why that is a deliberate, quantifiable trade.

Suppose there are ten million videos you could plausibly be shown. Your best ranking model takes, say, two milliseconds per video. Scoring all ten million takes five and a half hours. You have about 300 milliseconds.

So real systems are built as a funnel: a sequence of stages, each one cheaper per item and dumber than the next, each one throwing away most of what it sees.

  • Retrieval — millions → thousands. A single dot product per item, often not even that (see §3). Crude, but it runs over everything.
  • Ranking — thousands → hundreds. A neural network with real features. Expensive per item, affordable on a shortlist.
  • Re-ranking — hundreds → your screen. Diversity, freshness, policy, de-duplication. Looks at the set, not just each item alone (see §8).

The question a designer actually faces is: how much quality do you lose by letting a dumb model do the first cut? That is not a vibe — it is an integral, and you can move it around.

Recall@k is the share of the genuinely best k items that survive both cuts and reach your screen. Drag σ1 to 0.05 and the cheap model becomes clairvoyant; push it to 3 and retrieval is nearly a lottery.

What the model actually is. Each item has a true relevance r ~ N(0,1). Stage 1 sees r + ε₁ with ε₁ ~ N(0, σ₁²) and keeps the top K1; stage 2 sees r + ε₂ and keeps the top K2; the final re-ranker is assumed exact. The survival probability of an item with relevance r is then Φ((r−t₁)/σ₁)·Φ((r−t₂)/σ₂), and recall is that quantity integrated over the true top k. The page computes the thresholds t₁, t₂ by numerical quadrature and bisection every time you move a slider — nothing here is tabulated.
Where the toy simplifies. Real retrieval is not one noisy score: it is dozens of parallel sources (embedding neighbours, follows, hashtags, trending pools, fresh-content quotas) unioned together. Real relevance is not a single Gaussian number. And real stage costs depend on batching, caching and hardware. The shape of the trade-off — cheap-and-wide then expensive-and-narrow — is the part that transfers.

Feed Lab: be the user

The running example for the rest of the page. Skip, watch or like a few clips and watch a six-dimensional model of your taste assemble itself out of nothing.

There are six topics in this miniature world. Your taste vector t has one number per topic, and it starts at completely uniform — the system knows nothing about you. Every action you take nudges it. The system's guess at how much you'll like an item is just the dot product t·v of your taste with the item's topic vector.

Whatever state you leave this in follows you down the page: it is the user vector in §2, the taste-match feature in §6, the relevance scores in §8, and the starting condition in §9. The small bars in the header are it, live.

02

Everything is an arrow

Users and items live in the same space. Once they do, "does this person like this thing?" becomes a multiplication.

The founding move of modern recommendation is to represent a user and an item as vectors in the same embedding space — a list of numbers, where the dimensions are not chosen by a human but learned from data. Your taste vector in the Feed Lab is one, with six hand-labelled dimensions so you can read it. Real ones have 64 to 2048 dimensions and nobody knows what any single one means.

The payoff: affinity becomes the dot product.

t·v  =  t1v1 + t2v2 + … + tdvd  =  ‖t‖ ‖v‖ cos θ   // big when they point the same way

The dot product mixes two things: alignment (do they point the same way?) and magnitude (how long are they?). Sometimes you want only the first, and then you divide the magnitudes out:

cos(t, v) = t·v‖t‖ ‖v‖  ∈ [−1, 1]   // pure direction, ignores length

The difference matters. Magnitude usually encodes something like popularity or confidence, so a raw dot product quietly favours popular items, while cosine treats a niche item and a blockbuster as equals if they point the same way. Drag the arrows below and watch the two measures disagree.

Why high dimensions are strange

In two dimensions, two random arrows are often roughly aligned. In 512 dimensions, they are almost certainly perpendicular. For random Gaussian vectors the cosine between them has mean 0 and standard deviation 1/√d — so at d = 512, a typical cosine is about 0.044.

This is the reason embeddings work at all: in a big space there is room for millions of distinct "directions of taste" that barely interfere with one another. It is also why a cosine of 0.3 can be a very strong signal in a high-dimensional recommender, and why raw similarity numbers are meaningless without knowing d.

03

Finding neighbours without looking at everyone

Retrieval's real trick is not a better score. It is never computing most of the scores at all.

Stage 1 of the funnel needs the nearest few thousand items to your taste vector, out of hundreds of millions. Computing every distance is linear in the corpus — hopeless. So production systems use approximate nearest neighbour search, and accept losing a few true neighbours in exchange for touching a thousandth of the data.

Two families dominate. Locality-sensitive hashing (Indyk & Motwani, 1998) slices the space with random hyperplanes; each point gets a bit-string saying which side of each slice it fell on, and nearby points usually agree on most bits. Graph methods like HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin) instead build a navigable graph over the items and greedily walk towards the query. The demo below is LSH, because you can actually see the slices.

Each random line you add halves the expected bucket size — faster, but more true neighbours get separated from the query by an unlucky cut. Probing extra buckets (flipping up to p bits of the query's code) buys the recall back and costs you speed. That tension is the whole subject.

Push hyperplanes to 14 with probe radius 0: you scan well under 1% of the corpus, but move the query around and recall swings between 100% and 0% depending on whether an unlucky cut separates it from its neighbours. Probe radius 2 costs about 2.4% of the corpus and buys recall back to roughly 98%. A query sitting deep inside a dense cluster is easy; one near a boundary is where approximate search actually fails — which is why recall is always reported as an average over many queries, never a single one.

Where the toy simplifies. This is 2-D so you can see it; real LSH runs in hundreds of dimensions where the geometry is far less forgiving, and most large systems now prefer graph indexes (HNSW) or learned quantisation (product quantisation, ScaNN) over plain LSH. The recall and scan counts shown are computed exactly for this point set, not estimated.
04

People like you liked this

The oldest idea in the field, and still the backbone: predict from the crowd, not from the content.

Collaborative filtering needs to know nothing about what an item is. It only needs the big sparse matrix R of who interacted with what. If your column of ratings correlates with someone else's, their opinions are evidence about your future opinions.

Everything hinges on the similarity measure, and the three standard choices disagree in instructive ways:

cos(u,v) = Σi rui rvi√(Σ rui²) √(Σ rvi²)  // angle between rating vectors, blanks treated as 0
Pearson(u,v) = Σi∈I (rui−r̄u)(rvi−r̄v)√(Σ(rui−r̄u)²) √(Σ(rvi−r̄v)²)  // cosine after removing each user's mean
Jaccard(u,v) = |Iu ∩ Iv||Iu ∪ Iv|  // ignores the values, only what you both touched

Pearson exists because some people rate everything 4 and 5 and some rate everything 1 and 2; subtracting each user's mean asks "did you both deviate in the same direction?" rather than "did you both like it?". Jaccard exists because on most platforms there are no ratings at all — only implicit signals like watched / didn't watch.

Then the prediction is a similarity-weighted average of how your neighbours deviated:

r̂ui = r̄u + Σv∈N(u) sim(u,v) · (rvi − r̄v) Σv∈N(u) |sim(u,v)|

Where the toy simplifies. A real interaction matrix is roughly 99.99% empty, has hundreds of millions of rows, and contains implicit signals (watch time, skips) rather than stars. Nobody computes all-pairs user similarity at that size; item–item neighbourhoods are precomputed offline because items are fewer and their similarities change slowly. That observation — item–item is cheaper and more stable — is the practical reason the Amazon-style item–item recommender became an industry default.
05

Learning the hidden dimensions

Instead of comparing rows, factor the whole matrix — and let gradient descent invent the taste dimensions for you.

Neighbourhood methods compare users directly. Matrix factorization does something better: it assumes the big sparse matrix is secretly the product of two skinny ones.

R  ≈  P QT   // R is n×m, P is n×k, Q is m×k, and k is tiny
r̂ui = μ + bu + bi + pu·qi

Each user becomes k numbers; each item becomes k numbers; the prediction is their dot product, plus a global average and two bias terms that soak up "this user rates high" and "this item is widely liked". Those k numbers are latent factors — nobody labels them, they fall out of the fit. This is the family of methods that won the Netflix Prize, and it is still the conceptual core of the retrieval stage in §1: item embeddings on one side, user embeddings on the other, dot product in the middle.

Training minimises squared error on the cells you do observe, with a regularisation term that stops the factors from exploding:

L = Σ(u,i)∈observed (rui − r̂ui)²  +  λ(‖pu‖² + ‖qi‖² + bu² + bi²)

and gradient descent just walks downhill. For a single observed cell with error e = r − r̂:

pu ← pu + η(e·qi − λpu)     qi ← qi + η(e·pu − λqi)

That is the entire algorithm. Press play and watch it fill in the blanks.

Where the toy simplifies. Production systems rarely factor a static matrix. They train deep networks on streams of events, with embeddings for hundreds of sparse features, updated continuously — ByteDance's Monolith paper describes exactly this shift, including collisionless hash tables for embeddings and online training that keeps up with the traffic. Matrix factorization is the ancestor and the mental model, not the deployed system.
06

Ranking is a pile of probabilities

The ranker does not predict "how good is this". It predicts "how likely are you to do each specific thing" — and then somebody has to decide what those things are worth.

Ask a ranking model for one number and you have hidden a value judgement inside a neural network. So real systems do the opposite: they train a separate head for each action a user can take — like, comment, share, follow, watch to the end, hide, report — and each head outputs a probability.

The workhorse is the logistic (sigmoid) function, which squashes any real number into a probability:

z = w·x + b   // the logit: a plain weighted sum of features
σ(z) = 11 + e−z   // σ(0)=0.5, σ(2)≈0.88, σ(−4)≈0.018

Training uses cross-entropy loss, which is the negative log-likelihood of the data. It punishes confident mistakes brutally — being 99% sure of something that does not happen costs −ln(0.01) ≈ 4.6, while being merely unsure costs 0.69:

L(y, p) = −[ y·ln p + (1−y)·ln(1−p) ]

The gradient of cross-entropy with respect to the logit is exactly p − y — one subtraction. That astonishing simplicity is why logistic outputs plus cross-entropy is the default pairing in almost every ranker ever shipped.

Now the part that is a policy decision rather than a modelling one. You have five probabilities per candidate; the feed needs one ordering. Essentially every public description of a production ranker describes the same shape of answer — a weighted sum of predicted actions, an expected value where the weights say what each action is worth:

score(x) = Σa valuea · P(action a | x)   // an expectation, with hand-chosen prices

Watch the brevity column as you do it. In this toy the heads disagree about short clips on purpose — a fifteen-second loop gets finished and shared, a long tutorial gets comments and follows — so turning up "watch to the end" pulls the short items to the top and turning up "follow creator" buries them. That tension between completion-rate metrics and everything else is not invented for this page; it is one of the best-documented headaches in feed design.

Those weights are not learned from data. They cannot be — there is no ground truth for how many likes a share is worth. Somebody chooses them, and that choice is the product. Move them and watch the feed rearrange.

    07

    Exploit what works, explore what might

    A ranker that only shows what it already believes in can never discover it was wrong. The cure is a controlled, measurable dose of ignorance.

    A brand-new video has no history. A brand-new user has no taste vector. This is the cold start problem, and it is not solvable by better prediction — it is solvable only by spending some impressions on things you are uncertain about.

    Formalised, it is the multi-armed bandit: K arms with unknown success rates, one pull per round, maximise total reward. Performance is measured in regret — how much worse you did than always pulling the best arm:

    Regret(T) = T·μ* − Σt=1..T μa(t)   // lower is better; good algorithms grow like ln T, not T

    Three classic strategies, each a different answer to "how do I value not knowing?":

    • ε-greedy. With probability ε, pull a uniformly random arm; otherwise pull the best-so-far. Dead simple, and it never stops paying the exploration tax — regret grows linearly forever.
    • UCB1 (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer, 2002). Pull the arm maximising μ̂a + c·√(2 ln t / na) — optimism in the face of uncertainty. The bonus shrinks as an arm is pulled, so exploration automatically fades.
    • Thompson sampling. Keep a Beta posterior over each arm's rate, draw one sample from each, pull the argmax. Exploration falls out of the posterior's own width.

    The Beta–Bernoulli update is the cleanest piece of Bayesian machinery in the whole field. Start with Beta(1,1) — flat, total ignorance. After s successes and f failures the posterior is exactly Beta(1+s, 1+f). No integrals, no approximation: counting is the inference.

    The true rates are hidden from the algorithms but shown to you — that is the point of a simulator. Compare all three replays the identical reward tape through each algorithm, so the regret curves differ only by the decisions made.

    Expect a surprise: at the textbook constant c = 1, UCB1 loses badly here (regret near 130 at T = 2000 against about 20 for the others). That constant is chosen to make a worst-case guarantee hold, and on an easy five-arm problem it over-explores. Slide c down to 0.2 and UCB1 becomes the best of the three. Slide it to 0.05 and it collapses into pure greed and locks onto the wrong arm — the whole explore/exploit dial, in one slider.

    Where the toy simplifies. A feed is a contextual bandit with millions of arms, non-stationary rewards, delayed feedback and a different user every round — so nobody runs plain UCB1 on videos. But the accounting is the same, and the idea that a deliberate slice of traffic must be spent on uncertainty is universal. Note also that exploration is not charity: it is how the system avoids permanently mis-estimating things it stopped showing.
    08

    The top-k trap

    Sorting by score gives you the best item, then the item most like it, then the item most like that. Ten near-duplicates is a worse feed than eight good ones and two surprises.

    Every stage so far scored items independently. But a feed is consumed as a set, and the value of the fifth cooking video depends entirely on the four cooking videos above it. Score independently, sort, and take the top k, and you reliably produce a feed that is monotonous.

    The classic fix is maximal marginal relevance (Carbonell & Goldstein, 1998). Build the list greedily; at each step pick the item that maximises relevance minus its similarity to what you have already chosen:

    MMR = argmaxi∉S [ λ·rel(i) − (1−λ)·maxj∈S sim(i, j) ]

    λ = 1 is plain top-k. λ = 0 ignores relevance entirely and just picks whatever is least like the rest. The interesting behaviour is in between, and it is not subtle — slide it.

      09

      The system is also training you

      Your behaviour is the training data for the model that decides your behaviour. Close that loop and you get a dynamical system, with all the drift and lock-in that implies.

      Everything so far assumed a fixed world: items have relevance, users have taste, the model estimates them. But the estimate changes what gets shown, what gets shown changes what gets clicked, and what gets clicked becomes tomorrow's training data. There is no neutral observer anywhere in this diagram.

      The simulation below closes that loop with three dials. Popularity bias mixes log(1 + views) into the score, so things that were shown get shown (a rich-get-richer process — the same preferential-attachment mechanism that produces power-law popularity in almost every media system ever measured). Conformity controls how fast your taste vector moves toward whatever you were served. Interest decay pulls your taste vector back toward neutral each round, so an interest the feed stops serving quietly fades instead of persisting forever.

      Then there is the fourth dial, which is about what you are optimising. The simulation tracks two quantities: engagement (did the user interact?) and a "novelty satisfaction" proxy (how unlike the last ten items was this?). The score can be told to care about the second one. Watch what the first one does when you stop caring.

      Start from your Feed Lab taste. For a clean collapse set α = 0.6, β = 0, δ = 0, ε = 0 and step 100 rounds: topic entropy falls to 0.00 bits, the feed reaches only about 13 of the 120 items, and engagement sits near 0.97 — the bubble looks like a triumph from inside the metrics.

      Now raise ε to 0.25: entropy recovers to roughly 0.8 bits, reach jumps to about 80 items, and engagement falls to 0.90. That drop is the price, and it is why exploration is a product decision rather than a free lunch. Interest decay δ helps too but far more weakly — the feed simply re-teaches the taste it just decayed. And note what popularity bias β does: it raises topic entropy, because chasing global hits pulls you off your own track, while narrowing what the catalogue ever shows anyone. Two different pathologies, not one.

      Goodhart's law, on a slider. "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Turn the novelty weight to 0 and engagement climbs while satisfaction falls — the system is optimising the thing it can count. This is a toy, and a toy cannot prove anything about a real platform. What it can do is show that the divergence between a proxy and the thing you actually wanted is a structural property of closed-loop optimisation, not a bug someone forgot to fix. Researchers have studied this seriously: Chaney, Stewart and Engelhardt's "algorithmic confounding" work (RecSys 2018) shows recommenders trained on their own outputs increase homogeneity and can decrease utility.
      10

      How do you know it got better?

      Offline metrics that reward putting good things near the top, online experiments that are noisier than anyone would like, and a measure that is never quite the goal.

      Offline, you have a ranked list and some notion of which items were actually relevant. The standard metrics differ mainly in how much they care about position:

      • Precision@k — of the k shown, what fraction were relevant? Ignores order within the top k entirely.
      • Recall@k — of all relevant items, what fraction made the top k?
      • NDCG@k — discounts each item by 1/log₂(rank+1) and allows graded relevance, then normalises by the best possible arrangement. Rank 1 is worth about 2.6× rank 10.
      DCG@k = Σi=1..k 2reli − 1log2(i + 1)     NDCG@k = DCG@kIDCG@k   // IDCG = DCG of the perfect ordering

      Reorder the list and watch which metrics notice.

        Online, you split traffic and compare. The awkward truth is that feed metrics move by fractions of a percent, and noise is large. With a per-user click rate around p and n users per arm, the standard error of the observed difference is

        SE = √( pA(1−pA)n + pB(1−pB)n )     z = p̂B − p̂ASE

        Set the true lift to zero and run a few hundred experiments: about 5% will come back "significant" at p < 0.05.         And the metric is never the goal. Engagement is measurable; time well spent is not.         What is real here, and what is not

        Real: every number on this page is computed in JavaScript from the controls you moved — the Gaussian quadrature in §1, the sampled cosines in §2, the exact recall counts in §3, the similarity formulas in §4, the SGD updates in §5, the sigmoids in §6, the seeded bandit simulations in §7, the greedy MMR selection in §8, the closed-loop dynamics in §9, and the binomial draws in §10. Nothing is pre-scripted or tabulated. Random simulations use a seeded generator (mulberry32), so a given seed always replays identically.

        Not real: the models themselves. They are small enough to watch, which means they are far smaller than anything deployed. The specific feature values, weights and priors are invented for legibility. Nothing here reflects any company's internal system, and none of these numbers should be quoted as if it did.

        Unknowable: Instagram and TikTok have not published their ranking systems. Both have published qualitative overviews — Adam Mosseri's "Shedding more light on how Instagram works" (2021) and TikTok's newsroom post "How TikTok recommends videos #ForYou" (2020) — which describe signal types and the general shape, and no formulas. Anyone quoting exact weights is guessing.
