There is a number hiding in every sheet of paper you have ever used.

Not a metaphor. An actual number — one that had to be chosen before the first A4 page was ever cut, and one that cannot be written down completely. Let's fold our way to it.

Sheet 01 Meet the A4

Fold it in half. Then look again.

This is an A4 sheet — the one in every printer, notebook and photocopier outside of North America. Fold it top edge to bottom edge and cut along the crease, and you get A5.

Watch what happens to its shape. Not its size — everyone expects the size to change. Watch the shape.

Every fold produces a sheet that looks exactly like the one you started with, only smaller. A4, A5, A6, A8 — photograph them from far enough away and you could not tell them apart. That is not true of most rectangles.

Sheet 02 Why that is strange

Most rectangles do not survive being halved.

Take a square: 100 by 100. Fold it in half and you get 100 by 50 — a long, thin rectangle. Nothing like a square. Take a 16:9 television screen and halve it, and you get something noticeably squarer.

The A series does not do this. Every A size has the same width-to-height ratio as every other A size. Stack A4, A5 and A6 into the same corner and their far corners all land on a single straight line through the origin — the signature of shapes that are exact scale copies of each other.

That shared ratio is the whole trick. It is why a photocopier has a single button marked A4→A5 and the result never has awkward margins, and why enlarging a document is a clean scale-up rather than a re-layout.

The B series

Sometimes the jump between two A sizes is too big. The B series sits in between — B4 falls between A3 and A4. Posters, passports and book printing use them. Same shape rule, different starting size.

The C series

Envelopes. A C4 envelope is sized to swallow an A4 sheet unfolded; C5 takes an A4 folded once; C6 takes it folded twice. C sits between A and B, which is exactly the slack an envelope needs.

Sheet 03 The whole family

All of it comes from one big sheet.

The series starts at A0 — a single large sheet about the size of a small tabletop. Every other A size is A0 cut in half, again and again. Cut A0 and you have two A1s. Cut an A1 and you have two A2s.

Press Split and follow the shrinking rectangle. Each cut alternates direction, because each new sheet has flipped between portrait and landscape.

Highlighted: A0  ·  841 × 1189 mm

Notice that the highlighted rectangle never changes shape as it shrinks — it only alternates between standing up and lying down. Whatever ratio A0 was built with, halving preserves it perfectly. That is a very particular demand to make of a number. In a moment, you get to try to find it.

Sheet 04 Your turn

So which ratio does it?

Only one shape survives being halved. Your job is to find it — by feel, not by formula.

Call the ratio r = height ÷ width:

r > 1 is a portrait rectangle, standing up. r < 1 is a landscape one, lying down. r = 1 is a square.

Drag the slider to pick a shape. Then press Test this ratio: the rectangle folds in half, turns a quarter turn, and is laid over the shape you started with. If your ratio is the right one, the two land on top of each other exactly.

Why it has to be that number.

Now make the demand the whole system rests on: the half must be the same shape as the sheet it came from. So its ratio must be the original ratio.

Multiply both sides by r. Everything collapses into one line.

r is the number whose square is 2. That is what a square root is — and that is the name of this number: the square root of 2.

r = √2 = 1.41421356…

So the digits you saw on the slider were not a coincidence and not an approximation someone chose. A4 is 1 : √2 because that is the only shape that can be halved forever without changing. Every other ratio drifts.

And in the landscape zone: 0.70710678… is 1 ÷ √2. The same demand, written for a sheet lying down.

Sheet 06 Reality check

Real paper misses. On purpose.

√2 never finishes, but a paper mill has to cut somewhere. So every ISO size is rounded to a whole number of millimetres, and every real sheet is a hair off the ideal shape.

Here is how far off, size by size. Positive means the sheet is slightly taller than √2 demands; negative, slightly squatter.

Why the small sheets are worse. You might expect the error to shrink along with the paper. It grows. The reason is the ruler, not the mathematics: every size is rounded to the nearest whole millimetre, and half a millimetre is a rounding error of about 0.06% on A0's 841 mm side — but the same half millimetre is nearly 2% of A10's 26 mm side. The shape is exact. The measuring is what is coarse.

A0 through A10 are the real standard. A11 to A13 are shown here only as the halving pattern continued on paper — they are not ISO sizes, and you cannot buy them.

Sheet 07 Five things worth knowing

Notes from the edges of the system.

1 m²

A0 is a size as well as a shape. Its area is defined as exactly one square metre, and there is only one pair of sides with the √2 ratio that multiplies out to 1: 0.8409… m by 1.1892… m. Rounded to whole millimetres — 841 × 1189 — the real sheet lands at 0.99995 m². Every other A size is that square metre halved again and again; A4 is one sixteenth of it.

1786

The idea is older than the standard. The physicist Georg Christoph Lichtenberg described the halving ratio in a letter in 1786. Turning it into a system took another 136 years: Walter Porstmann worked out the full family, and Germany published it as DIN 476 in 1922. It went international as ISO 216 in 1975, and is now the paper of nearly every country outside the United States and Canada.

26 × 37 mm

Why the standard stops at A10. A8 is already down in business-card territory; A10 is a quarter of that again, roughly a postage stamp. Below it a sheet is too small to fold, print or handle, and the whole-millimetre rounding starts costing more than two percent of the shape. The halving pattern runs on for ever — that is what A11 to A13 in the chart above are — but the standard stops where paper stops being paper.

141%

The photocopier gives it away. Enlarging A4 to A3 means multiplying both sides by √2, so the button says 141% — √2 rounded off for a control panel. The reduce button says 71%, which is 1 ÷ √2. Two buttons, one number, hiding in plain sight next to the paper tray.

6 semitones

And one you can hear. Twelve equal semitones make an octave, which doubles the frequency — so six semitones multiply it by exactly √2. That interval is the tritone. Draw a square and its diagonal is √2 too. The number is not a paper thing; paper is just where it hides in your desk drawer.

Sheet 08 Where next
Where did this strange number come from — and why does it break the world of fractions?

√2 does not just run on for ever. It cannot be written as a fraction at all: no two whole numbers, however enormous, have a ratio that lands on it exactly. That discovery is said to have shaken the school of Greek mathematics that found it.
