      Every length is a ratio

      Remember the sheet of paper that folded onto itself perfectly, its long side matching its short side twice over? That number was hiding something, and it took the Greeks a few centuries to notice.

      Around 500 BCE, the followers of Pythagoras believed something beautiful about the world: that whole numbers explain everything. Not just counting, but music, astronomy, and above all measurement. Give them any two lengths and they were certain you could find a small unit that measures both exactly — so many of them in the first, so many in the second. Every length was a ratio of whole numbers.

      That belief is still hiding in the word we use today. A rational number is not a sensible one. It is a ratio one: a number you can write as with and whole numbers and .

      And the fractions really do look like enough. Watch what happens to a plain number line as you allow bigger and bigger denominators.

      Hold on to that feeling of a full, complete line. In the next scene we go looking for one specific point on it — and the hunt does not end the way anyone expected.

      The man in the water

      The Pythagoreans were not a maths department. They were a secretive brotherhood with rules about food, silence, and loyalty, for whom “all is number” was closer to a religious commitment than a research programme. A proof that some perfectly ordinary length is not a ratio was not an interesting result. It was a crack in the foundation of the world.

      The name attached to that crack is Hippasus of Metapontum, a Pythagorean of the fifth century BCE. According to the stories, he discovered incommensurability — or revealed it to outsiders — and did not survive the consequences. In one version the brotherhood drowned him at sea. In another he was cast out and drowned later, by the gods, for divulging what should have stayed hidden. In a third the secret he betrayed was not at all, but the construction of the dodecahedron.

      How much of this is true?

      Very little of it can be verified, and historians actively dispute it. No contemporary source describes the drowning: the accounts we have were written by Iamblichus and others some 700 to 900 years after the fact, and they contradict each other on who Hippasus was, what he revealed, and how he died. Some historians read the story as a later moral fable about betraying a school's secrets rather than a record of events.

      What is not disputed is the mathematics. Incommensurability was discovered in the Greek world sometime in the fifth century BCE, and the proof in the previous scene — or a close geometric relative of it — is genuinely ancient. Treat the drowning as legend. Treat the proof as fact.

      What actually broke

      Strip away the drowning and a real crisis remains. To see it, you have to notice what the Greeks did not have.

      They had no number . They had no decimals at all, and no notion of an irrational quantity as a number in its own right. What they had were whole numbers, and ratios of whole numbers, and geometric magnitudes — lengths, areas, volumes. Their entire theory of proportion rested on the assumption that any two magnitudes of the same kind are commensurable: that some common unit measures both a whole number of times.

      The diagonal and the side of a square are not commensurable. No unit, however small, fits a whole number of times into both. And that did not just add an awkward exception — it invalidated the proofs. Any theorem about similar triangles or proportional areas that had been established by counting common units was now resting on a claim known to be false in general. A large part of established geometry was suddenly unproved.

      The repair took roughly a century, and it came from Eudoxus of Cnidus in the fourth century BCE. His theory of proportion — preserved as Book V of Euclid's Elements — defines what it means for two ratios of magnitudes to be equal without ever counting a common unit. Instead it compares them against all whole-number multiples at once: the ratios are equal when every possible whole-number comparison falls the same way for both. That definition works perfectly well for incommensurable magnitudes, and it rescued the geometry.

      It also settled the direction of Greek mathematics for centuries. If arithmetic could not be trusted to describe magnitude, then geometry would be the rigorous foundation, and quantity would be handled by construction rather than by calculation. The number line as a continuous line of numbers — the thing you were sliding along in scene 1 — would not be built properly until the nineteenth century.

      One footnote worth knowing: Eudoxus' definition of equal ratios is startlingly close to the definition of a real number that Dedekind gave in 1872. The repair job outlasted the empire that commissioned it.
