      So what is the best you can do?

      The bound

      Every weighing has exactly three possible outcomes: left-heavy, right-heavy, or balanced. So w weighings can tell apart at most 3w different situations. To be guaranteed to pin down the heavy coin among n candidates you need 3w ≥ n.

      3w ≥ n  ⟹  w ≥ log3 n  ⟹  wmin = ⌈log3 n⌉

      The strategy that reaches the bound

      At every step, split the coins that are still in play into three groups as close to equal as possible — sizes differing by at most one coin — with the two pans holding equal counts. Whatever the scale does, you are left with roughly a third of what you had. That is exactly the "divide by 3 each time" that ⌈log3 n⌉ describes.

      The key word is current. The optimal split is recomputed from the group you have left, not locked to the ratio you opened with. This lab deliberately keeps applying your round-1 ratio so you can see the difference: an opening like 4 / 4 / 1 on nine coins can cost an extra weighing on the unlucky branch (4 → 2/2/0 → 1/1/0 is three weighings), while opening 3 / 3 / 3 never needs more than two.

      Why halving wastes information

      A binary split — about half on each pan, nothing aside — throws away the "balanced" outcome. Balanced can only ever point at the coins that never touched the scale, and in a half/half split that group is empty or nearly so. You are paying for a three-outcome instrument and collecting two outcomes. A three-way split cuts the candidates to about n/3 per weighing; halving cuts to about n/2, and log2 n is always bigger than log3 n.

      The pattern in base 3

      Each extra weighing multiplies the number of coins you can handle by three. Three coins in one weighing; nine in two; twenty-seven in three.

      A footnote for the curious: equal thirds is optimal for the guaranteed number of weighings. Occasionally a lopsided split has a slightly better average while still matching that guarantee — with 5 candidates, 1/1/3 averages 1.6 weighings against 2/2/1's 1.8, and both are never worse than 2.
